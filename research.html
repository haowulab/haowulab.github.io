Learning, Modeling, and Simulating Biological Systems

Biological systems are complex, dynamic, and spatially organized. Our research develops computational methods and AI models to understand how biological systems are organized, evolve, and respond to perturbations across molecular, cellular, spatial, and temporal scales.

We seek to move beyond static measurements toward computational models that can represent biological organization, reconstruct tissue structure, and ultimately predict how biological systems change over time.

Research Programs
01

Spatial & Single-Cell Biology

Understanding how cells are organized and interact within tissues

We develop computational methods for extracting biological structure from single-cell and spatially resolved molecular measurements. Our work addresses cell identity, cellular heterogeneity, spatial organization, and molecular variation across tissues and biological conditions.

  • Cellular states and cell types: statistical methods for cell-type annotation, clustering, rare-cell detection, and cell type-specific inference.
  • Spatial representation learning: learning representations that capture local molecular context and spatial organization in tissue.
  • Spatial molecular prediction: integrating histology and spatial omics to recover and predict molecular features of tissue.

02

Biological Representation & AI

Learning representations that capture biological context

Biological measurements are high-dimensional observations of underlying cellular and molecular systems. We develop representation learning approaches that capture relationships among genes, cells, tissues, and biological contexts, with the goal of building more generalizable models of biology.

  • Gene representation learning: learning distributed representations of genes from their molecular and biological context.
  • Context-aware biological representations: modeling how the meaning and behavior of genes and cells depend on cellular, spatial, and tissue context.
  • Multimodal biological AI: integrating molecular measurements, imaging, and other biomedical data to build richer representations of biological systems.

03

Spatial-Temporal Biology

Modeling how tissues evolve across space and time

Most molecular assays provide snapshots of biological systems, while biological processes unfold continuously through space and time. We aim to develop computational models that reconstruct spatial organization and infer how cells and tissues transition between biological states.

  • 3D tissue reconstruction: reconstructing continuous tissue organization from serial spatial measurements and histological observations.
  • Spatial field modeling: learning continuous representations of tissue structure and its spatial variation.
  • Cell and tissue fate prediction: predicting how cellular states and tissue organization evolve over time, including processes such as tissue remodeling and cancer progression.

Emerging work

3D tissue reconstruction · spatial field modeling · cell and tissue fate prediction
04

Clinical Trial Design & Real-World Evidence

Using computation to improve how clinical evidence is generated

We are exploring computational approaches that connect biomedical data with clinical development. A central goal is to build models and simulators that can use real-world evidence and prior clinical knowledge to understand trial populations, treatment effects, and sources of trial failure.

  • Clinical trial simulation: computationally modeling trial populations, endpoints, treatment effects, and sources of uncertainty to support trial design.
  • Real-world evidence: integrating heterogeneous clinical data to characterize patient populations and inform clinical development.
  • Learning from failed trials: developing computational frameworks to analyze why trials succeed or fail and to explore alternative design strategies before costly prospective studies.
Long-term Vision

Toward Biological World Models

Our long-term goal is to build computational models of biological systems that can reconstruct, simulate, and predict biological states across space and time.

We envision models that integrate molecular measurements, cellular organization, tissue architecture, and temporal dynamics to create computational representations of biological systems. Such models could provide a foundation for simulating biological processes and exploring how tissues respond to perturbations.

Foundations in Statistical Genomics

Our current work builds on a long-standing foundation in statistical genomics. Earlier research established statistical frameworks for single-cell omics data analysis, differential analysis, signal deconvolution, and modeling heterogeneous molecular data, providing methodological foundations for modern computational biology.

DSS — Dispersion Shrinkage for Sequencing data

Our most widely used method series, built on empirical Bayes shrinkage of dispersion parameters. The RNA-seq dispersion estimator was adopted by DESeq2, one of the most widely used differential expression tools, and the framework was extended to BS-seq for differential methylation analysis at both single-locus and region level.

2013, Biostatistics — A new shrinkage estimator for dispersion improves differential expression detection in RNA-seq data. The dispersion estimator was later adopted by DESeq2. 2014, Nucleic Acids Research — A Bayesian hierarchical model to detect differentially methylated loci from single-nucleotide resolution sequencing data. 2015, Nucleic Acids Research — Detection of differentially methylated regions from whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data without replicates. 2016, Bioinformatics — Differential methylation analysis for BS-seq data under general experimental design.

Other foundation tools

Cellcano — Cell type identification for scATAC-seq TOAST — Cell-type-specific analysis of bulk omics data InfiniumPurify — Cell-type deconvolution for DNA methylation data ChIPComp — Statistical analysis of ChIP-seq data
